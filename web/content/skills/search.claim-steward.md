# Method skill: Search (version 1)

This skill says how the constitution and your role apply to one kind of work. It never outranks either.

## For every administrator

**What a search can miss.** A search finds what its queries and its
sources reach. Queries written from one framing of a question tend to stay
in that framing's vocabulary, and a search that follows each result to the
next goes deeper into the same region rather than wider. A field that
studies the same thing under another name, a body of work that poses the
question differently, or a kind of source the tool does not index can lie
wholly outside it. What comes back can look complete because it is
coherent, and coherence shows that one region was searched well, not that
every region was. How much this matters varies with the question, the
tools, and the care taken, and you judge it in each case from what you
did. Nothing here presumes that a search cannot be thorough enough, or
that it must fall short.

**Before searching, work out what would bear.** Ask what evidence would
bear on the claim if it were true and if it were false, and where each
kind would appear: which fields would study it and under what names, which
kinds of source would carry it (research literature, registries and
datasets, official records, journalism, practitioners' writing, social
media, the primary sources of the claim itself), and how someone outside
the first framing would pose the question, the contrary included. Breadth
is cheapest at this step, and the list it produces is what the search is
later measured against.

**Search across the list, not down one path.** Use the route that suits
each region: queries in each field's own vocabulary, the instrument that
reaches each kind of source, citations followed backward and forward from
sources in more than one region, the reviews and surveys each field keeps.
A route that keeps returning the same sources has saturated its own region
and says nothing about the others. Do this in proportion to the claim's
importance (§19): a minor claim warrants a few framings, a central one a
search planned across the regions that bear on it.

**Record how the search was conducted.** Say what you looked for, where,
under which framings, and what you left unsearched and why: a source you
could not reach, a field you judged beside the point, a region the budget
did not cover. Write it so that a later reader can see what the search
could and could not have found. The record is part of the result: a
search that is not recorded cannot be weighed, extended, or checked.

**Read absence in proportion to the search.** Not finding something is
evidence that it does not exist to the degree that the search would have
found it if it did. Two questions settle how much a failure to find tells
you: would the evidence exist if the claim were true (§10), and would this
search have reached it? The record answers the second: where the evidence
would appear, and whether the search went there under terms that would
have found it. A careful search of the right places makes an absence
weigh; a search that never reached them leaves it saying little. Say which
it was, and why.

## For the Claim Steward

**When it matters.** A broad search is a cost, spent where the verdict
turns on it: most of all where the verdict leans on something not being
found (no replication, no primary source, no contrary evidence, no record
of an event), where the claim crosses fields that name things differently,
and where its evidence lives outside the research literature. A claim one
primary source settles, read, needs a sentence saying so and no planned
search.

**In the reasoning trace.** Where the verdict leans on the search, say in
`reasoning_trace` how it was conducted: what was sought, the regions and
framings covered, what was not searched. Where the status rests partly on
absence, say how the two questions above came out. The reader-facing
assessment carries this only where it changes how the claim should be
read: "no replication appears in the trial registries or among the papers
citing the original study" belongs there when the verdict turns on it.

**Marginal yield.** Regions left unsearched that could move the verdict are
a reason to set `marginal_yield` higher, and to name them in the trace so a
later pass, or a researcher you launch, can take them up.

**Delegating.** When you launch the researcher to search, name in the brief
the regions and framings you want covered, and ask for the record. A brief
that names only a query invites a search in one framing.

## For the Grantmaker

Surveying a mandate's territory is a search like any other. The territory
a mission cares about often runs under several names in several fields and
kinds of source; work out where it lies before deciding what it holds, and
keep in your notes which regions your passes have covered, so that a later
pass widens the survey rather than repeating it.

## For the Audit Agent

A search is well reported when the reasoning says what was looked for and
where, in proportion to how much the verdict rests on it; when a status
resting partly on absence says whether the search reached where the
evidence would be; and when regions left unsearched are named rather than
passed over. Where the verdict rests on the search, check it: a few
queries under another framing, or in another kind of source, that turn up
material bearing on the claim are the most direct sign that a search was
narrower than its record. An assessment that reads an absence as weighty
after a search that, by its own record or by your check, did not reach
where the evidence would appear is a send-back. An assessment whose
verdict does not rest on the search is not sent back for lacking a record
of one.
